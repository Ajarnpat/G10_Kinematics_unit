Why can we predict motion?

In Lessons 1–5 you learned to describe motion using position, velocity, acceleration and graphs. In Lesson 7 we ask a deeper question: why does motion change?

Discuss: Who was Isaac Newton, and when was he alive?

Make a prediction, then reveal the answer.

Sir Isaac Newton (1642/43–1727) published Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica in 1687, describing his laws of motion. Newton's laws still explain an enormous range of everyday motion; more advanced models are needed at speeds close to the speed of light and at atomic scales.

In every simulation, choose a direction as positive. Think about the net force on one selected object, not simply whether a force exists somewhere in the scene.

01 · Newton's first law

A moving object does not need a net force to keep moving

Discuss: State Newton's first law.
If the net external force acting on an object is zero, it remains at rest or continues moving with constant velocity in a straight line (in an inertial reference frame). A nonzero net force is needed to change velocity, not simply to keep an object moving.
Why does everyday experience seem different? A moving toy car often slows down because friction and drag act on it. An applied force can balance those resistive forces, giving zero net force while the car continues moving steadily.

Pause and predict: If the forces on a rolling car balance, can it be moving? What happens to its velocity if friction becomes larger than the applied force?

02 · Newton's second law

Net force produces acceleration

Discuss: What connects net force, mass and acceleration?
The acceleration of an object is determined by the vector sum of the external forces acting on it and by its mass. Acceleration is always in the same direction as the net force.
Fnet = ma

Units: Fnet in newtons (N), m in kilograms (kg), a in metres per second squared (m/s²). 1 N = 1 kg·m/s².

Quick question — attempt before viewing the answer

What net force is required to keep a 2.5 kg block moving in a straight line at a constant speed of 5 m/s?

Correct answer: 0 N. Constant velocity means a = 0; therefore Fnet = ma = 0. Forces such as friction may still exist, provided they balance.

04 · Newton's third law

Every interaction involves two forces

Discuss: What is Newton's third law?
If object A exerts a force on object B, then object B simultaneously exerts a force on object A that is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction. The two forces are of the same interaction type and act on different objects.

05 · Real practicals and virtual alternatives

Test Newton's laws in the laboratory

Practical 1 · Pulling a block

Measure the block's mass. Use a force meter to pull it horizontally along the table at a constant speed. Record the applied force and identify all forces acting on the block.

Increase the applied force slightly, aiming for a constant pull. Describe the motion and use the measured forces to estimate acceleration.

Virtual alternative: use Simulation 1 above. Kinetic friction is approximately independent of speed in this simplified model.

Practical 2 · Dropping a paper cone

Make a simple cone. Drop it from about 2 m. Does its motion appear to approach terminal speed? How could you measure speed instead of relying on eyesight?

If terminal speed is reached, use Newton's second law to determine the drag force. Suggest a variable you could change to investigate terminal speed.

It is not guaranteed that a cone will reach terminal speed during a 2 m fall.
